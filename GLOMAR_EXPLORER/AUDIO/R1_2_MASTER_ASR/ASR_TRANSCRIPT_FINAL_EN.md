# Actual ASR transcript — internal QC only, not subtitle delivery

### A01-P01
A Soviet helicopter circles an American ship taking photographs. On the ship's helicopter deck, canvas-covered crates block the landing space. The vessel is supposed to be mining the ocean floor. Beneath it lies the real reason it was built, a sunken Soviet submarine.

### A01-P02
The CIA wants part of that wreck brought up from more than three miles down, but the ship cannot simply lift recognizable submarine wreckage over the side. It has to bring its cargo aboard without showing the observers what it has found.

### A01-P03
How do you hide a submarine recovery inside a ship that everyone can see?

### A01-P04
The answer required more than a convincing story about Howard Hughes. It had to be built into the ship.

### A02-P01
In March 1968, the Soviet submarine K-129 disappeared in the Pacific. Soviet search efforts failed to locate it. The United States subsequently found the wreck that gave American intelligence something far more valuable than a photograph, a location where Soviet military equipment might be physically recovered.

### A02-P02
American intelligence wanted access to Soviet weapons, communications security and naval systems. Whether any particular item could be recovered was another matter. The first obstacle was physical, the wreck lay beyond the reach of ordinary salvage.

### A02-P03
By 1970, the CIA and its contractors had settled on a recovery approach, lower a huge mechanical capture vehicle from a specially equipped ship, grip the target section and lift it. But a vessel capable of that operation would advertise its unusual purpose through almost everything built onto it.

### A02-P04
A tower for handling a long string of pipe, heavy lifting machinery, an opening through the centre of the hull. None of it could be explained as an ordinary cargo ship. The cover had to account for equipment that would actually be there doing real work at sea.

### A02-P05
Deep ocean mining supplied that explanation. Manganese nodules really existed on the seabed, and commercial interest in recovering them was real. Hughes agreed to provide the cover for this particular venture. A large experimental mining ship could plausibly need unusual machinery and spend long periods working over deep water.

### A02-P06
The machinery no longer contradicted the cover story. It supported it. The tower, the long stays over deep water, the lifting equipment all could belong to a mining operation. But the moment a piece of submarine appeared above the rail, that explanation would stop working.

### A03-P01
Sun Shipbuilding in Chester, Pennsylvania, built the Hughes Glomar Explorer. Its construction was not hidden inside a remote military base. The shipyard even advertised a family day around its launch in November 1972. The existence of the vessel could be public. Its connection to the submarine could not.

### A03-P02
Inside that visible hull was a much less ordinary arrangement. A large central well-opened through the bottom of the ship, Gates could close beneath it. The recovered section would enter this sheltered space from below rather than swing over a rail for everyone to see.

### A03-P03
Now put that ship in a moving sea. The hull rises and rolls while the load hangs far below. Those movements must not simply pass straight into the lifting system. A gimballed platform and heave compensation limited the motion reaching the machinery. The crew needed to control the load, not just pull harder.

### A03-P04
Installing the heavy assemblies required a floating crane, the Sun 800, contemporary shipyard photographs show the platform hanging over the unfinished vessel. The same crane helped place other large structures. The apparently excessive industrial equipment was needed to assemble the ship before it could perform its secret task.

### A03-P05
Even leaving the shipyard required an adjustment. In July 1973, part of the derrick had to be removed so the explorer could pass beneath a bridge on the Delaware River. Once clear, a floating crane put the section back. Before this machine could reach a Soviet wreck, it had to fit through the American infrastructure in its way.

### A03-CTA01
Subscribe to Hidden Industrial America for the real purpose behind America's strangest machines and the engineering that made them work.

### A03-P06
The ship had cleared the bridge. Could its hidden opening survive the sea? During trials, the crew would have to flood the well and open the gates beneath it. A failure there could stop the operation before the capture vehicle ever reached the wreck.

### A04-P01
A successful sailing trial could not answer that question. The recovery system needed its own trials, open the well, handle the pipe string and operate the heavy lifting machinery while the ship moved around it.

### A04-P02
By January 1974, the program was running against a deadline imposed by the Pacific. Planners wanted the recovery ship on station during the summer weather window. Miss it, and the attempt could have to wait another year. More testing offered information, but also consumed the time needed to use it.

### A04-P03
On January 23, the crew flooded the central well and began opening its gates during sea trials. Heavy surges moved through the well. The machinery operating the aft gate was damaged. Inspectors found damage to the seal, gear teeth and the support for the drive shaft.

### A04-P04
To close the damaged gate, the crew had to rig cables and use winches. The trials stopped. The next day the ship was back at Long Beach. The opening intended to conceal the recovered submarine had become a reason the recovery ship could not continue its tests.

### A04-P05
Repairs brought their own compromise. There was not enough time to move the vessel into a suitable dry dock. Divers performed inspections and some seal repairs in the water. One persistent small leak was accepted. The ship would go on with a puddle in part of the well rather than wait for a perfect repair.

### A04-P06
February brought repairs and better results. The pipe handling system worked satisfactorily, but integrated tests exposed more faults. Repairing one component was not enough, the equipment had to work together, and each new round of testing used more of the remaining season.

### A04-P07
Officials eventually waived a planned test to 12,000 feet, accepting a demonstration at a few thousand instead. They trusted the crew's ability to work around faults. Repeated tests would also wear the specialized system. They were accepting uncertainty to keep the mission moving. That does not establish why the wreck later broke apart.

### A04-P08
And one uncertainty could not be rehearsed away, the wreck itself. Nobody could reproduce its damaged structure in a trial. A working lifting system would still have to trust that structure with the journey upward.

### A05-P01
Before that lift, there was a smaller journey the CIA also had to conceal, getting the capture vehicle aboard. The well could hide a recovered submarine. It would be little use if the equipment going into it had already revealed the mission.

### A05-P02
Loading it over the side would expose its shape during the transfer. Instead, the capture vehicle was built under cover and carried in a specially designed barge, HMB1. The barge had a retractable roof. What looked from outside, like another part of a mining enterprise, concealed the equipment inside.

### A05-P03
The transfer used the water itself as concealment. The barge submerged in shallow water. The explorer positioned itself above it. The capture vehicle could then be lifted into the central well from underneath instead of being suspended in the open air between the two vessels.

### A05-P04
The ship did not need to hide a crane, swinging its secret cargo overhead. It took delivery through the bottom. Later, the wreck would have to enter through that same concealed opening.

### A05-P05
The visible ship was only part of the operation. To understand the transfer, follow the capture vehicle. Out of the covered barge, through the water, into the well. At no point did that route require displaying it over the rail.

### A05-P06
but the system still depended on operating a conspicuous ship in one place while keeping the object below it out of view. The cover explained what an observer could see. It could not make the observer leave.

### A06-P01
President Nixon authorized the recovery plan on June 7th, 1974. The mission began that June and the explorer arrived over the recovery site on July 4. The ship had reached the place that justified years of construction. Now the crew had to turn the equipment into a working recovery system.

### A06-P02
On July 18th, the Soviet ship Chajma approached the Explorer. Its helicopter made repeated photographic passes. Before the ship arrived, the mission director had ordered canvas-covered crates stacked on the Explorer's helicopter deck to prevent a landing. The crates could block the landing space, they could not stop the cameras looking down from above.

### A06-P03
The heavy load did not hang from a single ordinary crane cable. The crew built a supporting string of steel pipe, adding sections as the capture vehicle descended. The ship's lifting machinery handled the string at the surface. Far below the capture vehicle had to reach and engage the target section.

### A06-P04
The ship had to hold its position while managing that suspended system. Equipment faults kept interrupting the work. On July 25th, a report described frequent shutdowns, mostly involving the heavy lift sensors and controls. The problem was no longer whether the machinery could work in a demonstration. It had to keep working through an actual recovery.

### A06-P05
At the bottom, the capture vehicle had to straddle the target and close its gripping structure around it. The machinery could reach the wreck. The next test was whether the wreck could bear being lifted.

### A06-P06
Upward meant reversing the pipe-handling operation. The crew raised the string and removed sections at the top as the load climbed below. The finish line was the central well. Get the wreck inside, close the gates and examine it out of sight.

### A06-P07
The intended load was a section about 132 feet long, not the entire submarine. Gripping it was only the beginning. It had to survive the ascent as a load the capture vehicle could keep holding.

### A07-P01
The load began to rise. The crew was bringing a Soviet submarine section toward a ship built to swallow it unseen. But during the lift, the section broke apart. Part fell back to the seabed. The equipment could continue upward with what it still held. It could not bring back the load that had just been lost.

### A07-P03
The remaining portion was brought aboard. Among its contents were the bodies of six Soviet Submariners. They were given a formal burial at sea. The recording of that ceremony would later be handed to Russian President Boris Yeltsin by CIA Director Robert Gates in 1992.

### A07-P04
Exactly what intelligence came back remains only partly public. What is confirmed includes a recovered section and the six sailors. The papers describing weapons, cryptographic systems and documents explain the mission's objectives. They do not give us a complete account of its results.

### A07-P05
A November 1974 intelligence review describes the target as broken, with equipment and documents apparently scattered in a new debris field. None of those scattered items was specifically identifiable at that time. Officials still believed valuable material could be recovered. The failure had changed the target, not eliminated the reason to return.

### A07-P06
There was still a target worth returning for and a ship that had brought part of it up, but a second attempt needed something no workshop could supply, more time under the same cover story.

### A08-P01
On February 6, 1975, President Ford approved another recovery attempt. One day later, the Los Angeles Times published an account linking Hughes, the CIA and the submarine operation. The authorization to return and the public unraveling of the cover arrived almost together.

### A08-P02
The trail toward exposure had passed through a break-in at Hughes corporate offices in June 1974. Investigators and reporters were drawn toward the connections behind the mining venture CIA director William Colby had appealed to journalists to hold back the story. For a time, some did, keeping the secret now depended on decisions far beyond the ship.

### A08-P03
On March 18th, Jack Anderson's syndicated TV report broadcast the story nationally. The New York Times followed with a detailed account on March 19th. An observer no longer had to infer a hidden purpose from strange machinery. The connection between the ship and the Soviet submarine had been published.

### A08-P04
Once the purpose was public, another attempt would have to proceed without the mining cover that had protected the first mission. On March 28th, Colby argued that a second mission was inadvisable because the operation had been exposed. Ford approved termination of the program on June 16th.

### A08-P06
The Explorer was mothballed in August 1976. It later entered commercial drilling service. In 2015, a Transocean filing recorded a completed sale after the decision to sell it for scrap.

### A08-P07
The engineering had answered the opening question. A mining project explained the ship. A submerged transfer hid its equipment. The central well concealed what came back. The secret was built into the route the cargo took. Once that route's purpose was public, hiding the cargo was no longer enough.

### A08-P08
The first attempt ended with part of the target back on the ocean floor. The second never sailed. The explorer had found a way to bring Soviet wreckage aboard unseen. It had no way to make its published purpose secret again.
