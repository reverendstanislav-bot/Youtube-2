# Glomar Explorer — R1.1 EN

Status: OWNER REVIEW / SCRIPT LOCK HOLD. Spoken text only beneath paragraph IDs. Shorts excluded. Source and editorial notes are in separate files.

## ACT 01 — A public launch, a hidden cargo

### A01-P01
A champagne bottle breaks against the bow. Families have been invited to watch. At a Pennsylvania shipyard, a new vessel enters the water under Howard Hughes's name. Its advertised job is mining the ocean floor. The cargo it was really built to lift was supposed to stay out of sight.

### A01-P02
The CIA wants this ship to retrieve part of a Soviet submarine from more than three miles down. Getting hold of the wreck is only half the problem. Bringing recognizable submarine wreckage into public view could expose the operation.

### A01-P03
So how do you build an enormous recovery machine in plain sight, load its most revealing equipment without displaying it, and bring the result inside the ship unseen?

### A01-P04
The answer begins with a mining story. It ends with a problem that no crane could solve.

## ACT 02 — The lie that explained the machinery

### A02-P01
In March 1968, the Soviet submarine K-129 disappeared in the Pacific. Soviet search efforts failed to locate it. The United States subsequently found the wreck. That gave American intelligence something far more valuable than a photograph: a location where Soviet military equipment might be physically recovered.

### A02-P02
The hoped-for intelligence included communications security, weapons technology, navigation and other naval systems. Those were objectives, not a list of prizes eventually brought home. First, someone had to reach a wreck resting at a depth where ordinary salvage methods offered no ready solution.

### A02-P03
By 1970, the CIA and its contractors had settled on a recovery approach: lower a huge mechanical capture vehicle from a specially equipped ship, grip the target section, and lift it. But a vessel capable of that operation would advertise its unusual purpose through almost everything built onto it.

### A02-P04
A tower for handling a long string of pipe. Heavy lifting machinery. An opening through the center of the hull. None of it could be explained as an ordinary cargo ship. The cover had to account for equipment that would actually be there, doing real work at sea.

### A02-P05
Deep-ocean mining supplied that explanation. Manganese nodules really existed on the seabed, and commercial interest in recovering them was real. Hughes agreed to provide the cover for this particular venture. A large, experimental mining ship could plausibly need unusual machinery and spend long periods working over deep water.

### A02-P06
That made the deception stronger than a false name painted on the hull. The public story and the real mission required similar visible behavior. Both involved reaching the bottom and lifting something back. The critical difference was what the machine would bring up.

## ACT 03 — Build the secret into the ship

### A03-P01
Sun Shipbuilding in Chester, Pennsylvania, built the Hughes Glomar Explorer. Its construction was not hidden inside a remote military base. The shipyard even advertised a family day around its launch in November 1972. The existence of the vessel could be public. Its connection to the submarine could not.

### A03-P02
Inside that visible hull was a much less ordinary arrangement. A large central well opened through the bottom of the ship. Gates could close beneath it. The recovered section would enter this sheltered space from below rather than swing over a rail for everyone to see.

### A03-P03
The lifting system created another problem. A ship rolls, pitches and rises with the sea. A suspended load resists being dragged along with every movement. The gimbaled platform and heave compensation were built to limit how those motions reached the lifting equipment. Here, controlling movement mattered as much as generating lifting force.

### A03-P04
Installing the heavy assemblies required a floating crane, the Sun 800. Contemporary shipyard photographs show the platform hanging over the unfinished vessel. The same crane helped place other large structures. The apparently excessive industrial equipment was needed to assemble the ship before it could perform its secret task.

### A03-P05
Even leaving the shipyard required an adjustment. In July 1973, part of the derrick had to be removed so the Explorer could pass beneath a bridge on the Delaware River. Once clear, a floating crane put the section back. Before this machine could reach a Soviet wreck, it had to fit through the American infrastructure in its way.

### A03-P06
The hull, machinery and public explanation were coming together. Yet being seaworthy was not the same as being ready to recover a submarine. The ship could sail successfully while the most important parts of its mission remained unproven.

## ACT 04 — The ship fails before the mission

### A04-P01
The distinction appeared in the testing program. Builder's trials checked the vessel at sea. Later trials had to exercise the specialized recovery systems: pipe handling, lifting equipment, docking legs and the machinery that compensated for ship motion. Passing one stage did not settle the next.

### A04-P02
By January 1974, the program was running against a deadline imposed by the Pacific. Planners wanted the recovery ship on station during the summer weather window. Miss it, and the attempt could have to wait another year. More testing offered information, but also consumed the time needed to use it.

### A04-P03
On January 23, the crew flooded the central well and began opening its gates during sea trials. Heavy surges moved through the well. The machinery operating the aft gate was damaged. Inspectors found damage to the seal, gear teeth and the support for the drive shaft.

### A04-P04
To close the damaged gate, the crew had to rig cables and use winches. The trials stopped. The next day the ship was back at Long Beach. The opening intended to conceal the recovered submarine had become a reason the recovery ship could not continue its tests.

### A04-P05
Repairs brought their own compromise. There was not enough time to move the vessel into a suitable dry dock. Divers performed inspections and some seal repairs in the water. One persistent small leak was accepted. The ship would go on with a puddle in part of the well rather than wait for a perfect repair.



### A04-P06
Further trials in February showed improvement. The pipe-handling system operated satisfactorily, and lifting-system faults were repaired. But the integrated tests that followed brought more equipment problems and changes to the schedule. The project could not keep treating every setback as though unlimited development time remained.

### A04-P07
Senior officials accepted a demonstration at a few thousand feet and waived a planned test down to twelve thousand feet. The internal history records their confidence in the crew and the practical workarounds it could devise. It also notes that repeated testing would put wear on a system designed around a particular recovery operation.

### A04-P08
No rehearsal could establish how the real wreck would hold together. Its structural integrity and stability remained unknown. The equipment had to be judged ready without being able to reproduce the condition that might decide the result: how the damaged target behaved when lifted.

## ACT 05 — Load it from underneath

### A05-P01
The recovery system also had a concealed loading problem. During preparation, the capture vehicle had to pass from its covered construction area into the ship without being displayed in the open. Its transfer required an operation of its own.

### A05-P02
Loading it over the side would expose its shape during the transfer. Instead, the capture vehicle was built under cover and carried in a specially designed barge, HMB-1. The barge had a retractable roof. What looked from outside like another part of a mining enterprise concealed the equipment inside.

### A05-P03
The transfer used the water itself as concealment. The barge submerged in shallow water. The Explorer positioned itself above it. The capture vehicle could then be lifted into the central well from underneath, instead of being suspended in the open air between the two vessels.

### A05-P04
The barge concealed the equipment during loading. Once the capture vehicle was inside, the Explorer's hull and central well provided the sheltered route through which the recovered target could return. The loading system and the recovery system solved different parts of the same secrecy problem.

### A05-P05
This is why the ship's unusual features belonged together. The covered barge hid the transfer. The central well hid the load at the surface. The lifting system connected that concealed working space to the bottom of the ocean. Secrecy was a requirement shaping the machinery, not something added after construction.

### A05-P06
But the system still depended on operating a conspicuous ship in one place while keeping the object below it out of view. The cover explained what an observer could see. It could not make the observer leave.

## ACT 06 — Work while they watch

### A06-P01
President Nixon authorized the recovery plan on June 7, 1974. The mission began that June, and the Explorer arrived over the recovery site on July 4. The ship had reached the place that justified years of construction. Now the crew had to turn the equipment into a working recovery system.

### A06-P02
On July 18, the Soviet ship Chazhma approached the Explorer. Its helicopter made repeated photographic passes. Before the ship arrived, the mission director had ordered canvas-covered crates stacked on the Explorer's helicopter deck to prevent a landing. The crates could block the landing space. They could not stop the cameras looking down from above.

### A06-P03
The heavy load did not hang from a single ordinary crane cable. The crew built a supporting string of steel pipe, adding sections as the capture vehicle descended. The ship's lifting machinery handled the string at the surface. Far below, the capture vehicle had to reach and engage the target section.

### A06-P04
The ship had to hold its position while managing that suspended system. Equipment faults kept interrupting the work. On July 25, a report described frequent shutdowns, mostly involving the heavy-lift sensors and controls. The problem was no longer whether the machinery could work in a demonstration. It had to keep working through an actual recovery.

### A06-P05
The capture vehicle had to straddle the target and close its gripping structure around it. Photographs alone could not establish how every damaged part would behave under load. Reaching the wreck meant the equipment had arrived. Lifting it would be the test of whether the entire arrangement could bring its intended load back.

### A06-P06
For the return, the sequence reversed. The machinery raised the pipe string; sections came out at the top as the captured load rose below. The objective was to bring the target all the way into the central well, close the gates and examine it inside the vessel, screened from outside observation.

### A06-P07
The target was a section of the submarine, not the entire boat. The CIA's museum account describes a portion roughly 132 feet long. Even that limited objective meant entrusting a large damaged structure to the capture system for the whole journey upward. The wreck had to remain a recoverable load after the gripping machinery reached it.

## ACT 07 — What came back

### A07-P01
The wreck was rising. It still had to hold together until it reached the sheltered well. During the lift, the submarine section broke apart. Part of it fell back to the seabed. The crew could still recover what remained in the capture vehicle, but the intended target was no longer coming up as one load.


### A07-P03
The remaining portion was brought aboard. Among its contents were the bodies of six Soviet submariners. They were given a formal burial at sea. The recording of that ceremony would later be handed to Russian President Boris Yeltsin by CIA Director Robert Gates in 1992.

### A07-P04
Exactly what intelligence came back remains only partly public. What is confirmed includes a recovered section and the six sailors. The papers describing weapons, cryptographic systems and documents explain the mission's objectives; they do not give us a complete account of its results.

### A07-P05
A November 1974 intelligence review describes the target as broken, with equipment and documents apparently scattered in a new debris field. None of those scattered items was specifically identifiable at that time. Officials still believed valuable material could be recovered. The failure had changed the target, not eliminated the reason to return.

### A07-P06
So the next question was no longer how to build the ship. It was whether the same ship, already proven capable of a partial recovery, could make another attempt before anyone outside the operation understood its real assignment.

## ACT 08 — The deadline nobody could repair

### A08-P01
A second mission was proposed. On February 6, 1975, President Ford approved another recovery attempt. Then, the following day, the Los Angeles Times published an account connecting Hughes, the CIA and the submarine operation. The plan to return had reached presidential approval just as its cover began failing in public.

### A08-P02
The trail toward exposure had passed through a break-in at Hughes corporate offices in June 1974. Investigators and reporters were drawn toward the connections behind the mining venture. CIA Director William Colby had appealed to journalists to hold back the story. For a time, some did. Keeping the secret now depended on decisions far beyond the ship.

### A08-P03
On March 18, Jack Anderson's syndicated television report broadcast the story nationally. The New York Times followed with a detailed account on March 19. An observer no longer had to infer a hidden purpose from strange machinery. The connection between the ship and the Soviet submarine had been published.

### A08-P04
Once the purpose was public, another attempt would have to proceed without the mining cover that had protected the first mission. On March 28, Colby argued that a second mission was inadvisable because the operation had been exposed. Ford approved termination of the program on June 16.


### A08-P06
The Explorer was mothballed in August 1976. It later entered commercial drilling service. In 2015, a Transocean filing recorded a completed sale after the decision to sell it for scrap.

### A08-P07
The concealment worked as a chain: a plausible mining project, a covered transfer, and a well through the ship's hull. Together, they could keep the recovered cargo out of view. They could not preserve the mission's cover once its purpose had been published.

### A08-P08
The Explorer had reached the wreck and brought part of the target back. Officials still saw a reason to return for what remained. But before the ship could try again, its secret became public. The second mission was canceled. The recovery ended with the job unfinished.
