## Four Shorts — draft scripts (English narration, <=35 sec after voice QC)
SHORT 1 — A Missile Beneath the City
Beneath the hills near San Francisco, the U.S. Army once stored missiles underground. When the alert came, huge elevators could raise the weapons to the surface. This wasn't a single secret laboratory. It was one piece of a nationwide air-defense network built around America's cities. And one preserved site still exists today: SF-88.
SHORT 2 — A Nuclear Weapon for Defense
Nike Ajax used a conventional warhead. Then, in 1958, Nike Hercules entered service with a much more disturbing capability: it could carry a nuclear warhead. The idea was to destroy incoming bomber formations before they could reach a city. Cold War defense had reached a frightening paradox: a nuclear blast intended to prevent a nuclear attack.
SHORT 3 — Two Sites, One Missile
A Nike base wasn't just a missile launcher. In a separate control area, radar operators tracked the target and the missile. The missiles themselves could wait underground on another site, ready to rise on an elevator. These two installations had to operate as one system. A Cold War defense machine could be spread across a landscape that looked almost ordinary.
SHORT 4 — Why the Bases Closed
America built hundreds of Nike missile facilities to stop enemy bombers. But the nuclear threat changed. Intercontinental ballistic missiles flew very differently from aircraft. Ajax and Hercules were never designed as a complete answer to that problem. The nationwide system shrank. By 1979 the last U.S. Hercules site had closed. Some bases vanished. Others still stand.
