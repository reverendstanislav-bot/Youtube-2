# PROJECT PLUTO — STAGE 02: EDITORIAL ANGLE & HOOKS V1

Status: WORKING PROPOSAL — NOT OWNER/TITLE LOCK.
Story scope: source-grounded history of a cancelled engineering/defense megaproject. Intended long-form English US documentary (~16–22 min working target, to be validated against real script length).

## Recommended angle: IT WORKED — AND THEN THEY CANCELED IT
Central question: Why did engineers succeed in testing a nuclear-powered ramjet, only for the US defense establishment to abandon the missile it was supposed to power?

This is stronger than “the world's craziest missile.” The reversal is supported by the dated government record, not merely a sensational theory. Every major turn answers a question and opens the next.

## English opening candidate (not fact-locked narration)
“In May 1964, a machine in the Nevada desert did something the U.S. military had spent years trying to achieve. It used a nuclear reactor to power a jet engine. The test worked. But just weeks later, the Defense Department pulled the plug on the plan to fly it. So what had engineers actually built — and why did success end the program?”

Russian editorial translation:
«В мае 1964 года машина в пустыне Невады сделала то, над чем американские военные работали годами. Ядерный реактор стал источником энергии для реактивного двигателя. Испытание прошло успешно. Но всего через несколько недель Министерство обороны отказалось от планов поднять его в воздух. Что же инженеры на самом деле построили — и почему успех стал концом программы?»

## Provisional chapter spine
1. Cold-open the real Tory II-C in Nevada, test success and July reversal.
2. Define Pluto (nuclear ramjet research) versus SLAM (proposed missile). Introduce impossible engineering premise.
3. Why 1950s strategic planners wanted it: nuclear-age weapons choices. Carefully source designs as intended, not realized.
4. Nevada's enormous test infrastructure and Tory II-A proof in 1961.
5. The hard science that made Tory II-C a multi-year industrial materials problem, not a sketch of an airplane.
6. May 1964 success in simulated flight conditions; show actual source, engineering context.
7. Decision paradox: July 1 DOD rejection, strategy, alternatives, and risk; clearly separate competing explanations.
8. Aftermath: no flown SLAM, surviving primary evidence, technology demonstration versus strategic obsolescence.
