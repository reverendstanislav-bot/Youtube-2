## Subject and definitional split
Project Pluto was a Cold War US effort to create a reactor-powered ramjet for low-flying supersonic missiles. The proposed delivery vehicle was called SLAM — Supersonic Low-Altitude Missile. Project Pluto produced real ground-tested nuclear ramjet hardware; it did NOT culminate in a flight-tested or deployed SLAM missile. Avoid every image/text claim that conflates engine test, flight test, missile construction, and operational fielding. [P01,P02,P04]

## Why this can carry a film
The most counterintuitive moment is not an explosion or a failed test. It is the apparent victory: May 1964 Tory II-C ground runs reportedly met test objectives; within weeks the Defense Department abandoned the desired flight-test path. Central suspense: why does a demonstration that works still end a national weapons project? [P01,P02,P03]

## Grounded chronology
- 1957: Livermore began Pluto work for the AEC and US Air Force. [P02]
- 1960–61: Nevada Pluto test installations and remotely operated equipment developed to test reactor hardware under simulated flight conditions. [P05,P07,P08]
- May 14, 1961: Tory II-A ran during a 45-second first test according to Livermore's own historical account; later 1961 testing continued. [P02]
- Tory II-A established the feasibility of a high-temperature nuclear ramjet system but was not a flight-ready SLAM airframe. [P04,P05]
- March 25, 1964: Tory II-C achieved first criticality in its test vehicle. [P01]
- May 12, 1964: intermediate-power test simulating flight at about Mach 2.8 at 10,000-foot altitude. [P01]
- May 20, 1964: full-power ground test at simulated sea-level conditions and Mach 2.8; the AEC report calls May tests successful. [P01]
- July 1, 1964: DOD notified the AEC that a Pluto flight-test objective would not be pursued; remaining tests canceled and project phased out. [P01]
- No flight test of SLAM occurred. [P01,P11]

## The engineering obstacle — explain, don't turn into a build manual
Unlike a conventional jet engine, the intended engine drew heat from a nuclear reactor to energize incoming air as the aircraft traveled at high speed. The historical challenge combined extreme heating, structural materials and reactor control while simulating supersonic conditions on the ground. Follow period reports and use simplified non-operational motion diagrams. [P04,P05,P09]

## The facility as a protagonist
Nevada's Pluto site needed major industrial testing infrastructure — air handling, test handling, rail transport and post-test disassembly. Library of Congress HAER inventories provide several dozen historically documented facility photos and building histories: this is a distinctive physical world, not generic “nuclear missile stock footage.” [P07,P08]

## Why cancel? Differentiate verified decision from hypotheses
Fact: DOD opted against pursuing a flight-test objective and the AEC phased out Pluto in 1964. [P01]
Interpretive context for investigation: the rising usefulness of ICBMs, strategic questions about the proposed vehicle, and program expense / risk are plausible elements mentioned in retrospective sources [P10,P11]. Do not imply a single decisive cause, a reactor defect, a verified nuclear fallout trail, or a documented disaster without direct records.

## Strong chapter reveals
1. A photograph of a real gigantic nuclear ramjet on the rails in Nevada. Real test machine — not a film prop.
2. It was only an engine, not an operational missile.
3. A flight-speed engine test required a special industrial facility and remote operations.
4. May 1964: engineering milestone, not battlefield breakthrough.
5. July 1964: the Defense Department said no to flying it.
6. The ending reframes the story: engineering feasibility does not equal strategic value.

## Editorial danger list
- “They flew a nuclear missile over America” — false.
- “The test reactor was detonated” — false.
- “It would have run forever” — unsupported exaggeration.
- “Radioactive exhaust poisoned cities during tests” — not established and confuses test and hypothetical wartime use.
- “No safeguards or reactor shutdown” — do not generalize from sensational secondary narratives.
- “500 megawatts of thrust” — unit/category error.
- “Nuclear-powered space rocket project” — confuses Pluto with Rover/NERVA.
- Every hypothetical airframe visualization MUST read as a CONCEPT / AI RECONSTRUCTION, never HISTORICAL SOURCE.
