## CH01 — THE CITY THE ARMY HAD TO KEEP DIGGING OUT

<!-- evidence ARMY65_FIG28_FIG29_P41;AGU16;NIELSEN14 -->

In nineteen sixty-three, the United States Army had a strange problem in Greenland.

The space inside its newly built underground city was shrinking.

Not to an enemy. Not to an avalanche. The ceiling itself was slowly coming down.

This was Camp Century: a working settlement hidden beneath the surface of the Greenland ice sheet. There were sleeping quarters, workshops, medical facilities, heated corridors, and a nuclear power plant. Tracked vehicles could travel along an underground main street.

But the Army's own measurements told another story.

The Army documented one section of its main passage losing roughly twelve percent of its cross-sectional area per year. In a separate side passage, the figure was seventeen percent. Those were measured examples, not identical rates for every tunnel.

The Army's engineers estimated that keeping this one camp's tunnels at their original dimensions would require cutting and removing around twenty thousand cubic yards of snow every year.

That is not the amount actually excavated in every year. It was the Army's estimate of what maintenance would demand.

The Army's surviving film records the machines, trenches, and extraordinary construction effort. The published engineering report shows how the arches and rooms were assembled beneath the surface.

Everything looks finished.

But the biggest construction job might have been the one that never ended.

Now for the part the public movie did not tell in full.

As the research program developed, military planners proposed a much larger system: buried railways and launching locations for hundreds of nuclear-armed missiles.

The proposed arsenal was never built. The base was.

So the mystery isn't whether the Army knew how to build beneath the ice. The films prove that it did.

The question is why building one working city did not lead to the enormous underground military system being imagined.

And the first clue lies in a journey of one hundred thirty-eight miles, through a place without a road.

## CH02 — AN ENTIRE CITY ARRIVED ON SLEDS

<!-- evidence ARMY65_P31_P32;SCI24;1951_DEFENSE_CONTEXT -->

The year is nineteen fifty-nine.

On the northwest Greenland ice sheet, there is no construction site as most people would recognize one. No trucks are arriving on pavement. No concrete mixers line up at a gate.

The buildings do not exist yet. There is no paved road leading to the site.

Greenland is part of the Danish realm, and the United States operates in the region under defense arrangements. Geography, however, has its own rules.

The project uses Camp Tuto, a staging area near the edge of the ice. From there, men and machinery must travel far inland.

The distance to Camp Century is approximately one hundred thirty-eight miles.

Tractors pull long sled trains across the snow, carrying the materials needed to create an underground settlement. Some loads are so awkward that the Army has to make special sleds for them.

It is the image that makes the construction story so tangible: a small line of heavy machines moving across an almost featureless white horizon.

But the cargo tells you what is really happening.

Steel, timber, heating equipment, prefabricated rooms, electrical gear, fuel, tools, and equipment heavy enough to require its own transport arrangements.

In the first season, roughly one thousand short tons of materials move from Camp Tuto toward the site.

In nineteen sixty, the total is another four thousand six hundred.

About five thousand six hundred short tons in two construction seasons.

And every machine hauling that cargo needs fuel of its own. Spare parts have to follow the same route. When conditions change, a trip can become another test of the transport system.

This is the first hidden cost of building in the Arctic: before your machines can solve the problem, you must build a way to bring the machines there.

Yet by October nineteen sixty, the Army has created a functioning camp.

The achievement looks almost impossible.

Until you see how they actually built it.

They did not blast their way through a mountain. They did not drill an enormous tunnel through solid glacial ice.

They took advantage of something easier to cut than rock.

And much harder to trust.

## CH03 — HOW DO YOU BUILD A ROOF FROM SOMETHING THAT MOVES?

<!-- evidence ARMY65_FIG12_TO_26;SCI24 -->

The central construction machine at Camp Century was called the Peter snow miller.

In the original footage, it looks less like ordinary snow removal equipment and more like an industrial machine trying to eat its way through an endless white wall.

It cuts a long trench into compacted snow.

The next step is deceptively simple: build inside the trench, cover it with a roof, then put snow back on top.

This is cut-and-cover construction. But unlike an ordinary city, the material above and beside the buildings is not stable earth.

It's snow and firn, constantly responding to pressure and temperature.

The Army tested different forms of roof.

In some locations, the trench walls were undercut so that compacted snow could form an arch. In others, metal arches helped support the larger openings.

The engineers were trying to make the surrounding snow part of the structure, without allowing it to destroy the structure.

For the broad reactor building space, the Army needed a trench around forty feet wide.

That is not just a larger version of a corridor. Spanning a much wider area meant taking on new loads, new forms, and new opportunities for something to go wrong.

Some of the first failures were quietly revealing.

In certain sections, temporary roof forms were removed before the snow above them had properly strengthened.

The openings began to deform.

The solution was sometimes to leave the support forms where they were.

Those are decisions a construction crew has to make in the moment, long before a promotional documentary can show a finished city.

But the buildings were only the beginning.

Under the snow, Camp Century developed a main street and a network of smaller passages. The layout included a dining hall, barracks, a medical facility, workshops, storage, recreational rooms, even a chapel and theater.

The camp was designed for as many as two hundred people.

From the outside, a blank field of snow. Inside, an operating settlement.

There was one important compromise.

Every square foot of roof above the camp was also a load pressing down on the empty space below.

And the camp would be adding something that made the surrounding environment even more complicated.

Heat.

Not just from the people living there, but from a machine that cost more than most of the city around it.

## CH04 — THE MOST EXPENSIVE ROOM WAS A NUCLEAR POWER STATION

<!-- evidence ARMY65_P31_P41_P47_P51;USACE_PM2A -->

The Army had a supply problem it could not escape.

Keep a remote settlement warm with diesel generators, and every season brings another long convoy carrying fuel.

Or install a machine that can produce a large amount of power without the same continuous fuel deliveries.

That second idea became PM-two-A, Camp Century's portable nuclear power plant.

It wasn't a bomb. It was an electrical and heating system for the camp.

The price was extraordinary.

A nineteen sixty-five Army report gives Camp Century's actual construction cost as seven point nine two million dollars.

The nuclear power plant alone accounted for about five point seven million.

Those are period dollars. And they mean that the plant consumed the great majority of the initial project cost.

The reactor arrived as another set of heavy loads hauled across the ice. Engineers had to assemble it in the prepared underground plant space.

Its reactor produced heat on the scale of ten megawatts, while the electrical output supplied to the camp was roughly one and a half megawatts.

Those figures measure different things. Reactor heat is not the same as electricity delivered to the buildings.

At Camp Century, the heat itself was useful.

The base required water. Engineers developed systems to melt snow and ice and recover usable water from beneath the surface.

But this creates another problem.

Where do you put the water after people have used it?

The Army's engineers built underground disposal sumps. One was placed too near living quarters during a rush to finish before winter.

The odors reached the occupied buildings.

And that wasn't the worst consequence.

Warm liquid moving into the snow changed the ground nearby. The original report described the floor near the disposal area settling by roughly four feet, forcing the removal of two nearby buildings.

The camp had literally altered the material holding up part of its own infrastructure.

Meanwhile, the nuclear plant's turbine-generator developed repeated mechanical troubles. Keeping the city supplied meant solving ordinary industrial failures in a profoundly unusual place.

The reactor was a real technical achievement.

But the more systems the Army put underground, the more there was to maintain.

And there was a reason planners were willing to accept such enormous expense and complexity.

They weren't thinking only about a research camp.

They were imagining a weapon that might never have to stay in the same place twice.

## CH05 — THE 600 MISSILES THAT NEVER ARRIVED

<!-- evidence NIELSEN14_P445;AGU16;PETE08 -->

The Army made films about Camp Century.

The cameras showed construction equipment, comfortable rooms, Arctic research, and an American engineering triumph.

Much of that was true.

The camp was real. The scientific work was real. The challenges of living beneath the ice were real.

But military research connected it to another proposal that the public film did not fully explain.

Project Iceworm.

The idea was to use Greenland's ice sheet to conceal a vast network of mobile ballistic missile positions.

A fixed missile base can be mapped. A missile moved through an underground network would be harder to locate, at least in theory.

Historical research describes plans for roughly six hundred missiles and more than two thousand possible launching positions.

Not six hundred missiles actually delivered to Greenland.

Not two thousand completed underground silos.

Those figures belong to a proposed military system.

Historical research describes a proposed network across approximately one hundred thirty-five thousand square kilometers.

And the Army had one experimental component that made the proposal sound less imaginary.

The Army had installed an experimental subsurface railway at Camp Century, but this was only a research-scale installation.

That was real.

But a short experimental track is not a functioning continent-sized missile transport network.

Every passage would have to remain open. Every point would require maintenance. Trains, machinery, power, communications, and crews would all need support.

And those demands would be multiplied thousands of times over.

There was another obstacle.

The United States was building on Greenland, within the Kingdom of Denmark. An American research installation did not automatically provide political consent for an enormous hidden nuclear weapons complex.

Within the U.S. military, the Air Force and Navy were also developing other strategic missile systems.

The Army proposal faced rival technologies, questions about its military purpose, and problems no engineering demonstration could resolve.

According to a later scientific study, the proposal was rejected in nineteen sixty-three.

The missile network was never completed, and the weapons were never deployed.

But even if a government had decided to finance the entire scheme, the base had already produced a warning.

Cutting the tunnels would have been only the beginning. Keeping a vast network usable would be another challenge, alongside the military and political objections.

## CH06 — THE BILL THAT NEVER STOPPED COMING

<!-- evidence ARMY65_TABLEII_FIG28_FIG29_P41_P44;SCI24 -->

By the early nineteen sixties, Camp Century's engineers were measuring something their cameras couldn't make glamorous.

The shrinking of empty space.

A snow trench can look perfectly solid. Then, month by month, the roof settles.

The walls creep inward because the snow and firn around them are deforming under pressure.

The Army knew about creep. This wasn't the discovery of a new physical law.

The problem was the scale of what maintaining a city inside that moving material actually required.

Look at two diagrams in the Army's nineteen sixty-five report.

They show the same tunnel sections at different dates.

The difference isn't subtle.

In the main passage, the report illustrates one section losing about twelve percent of its cross-sectional area a year. A side passage is documented at roughly seventeen percent.

The Army also reported average annual vertical closure of about sixteen inches in the main passage, and about thirty-four inches in side trenches. These are clearance losses, not additional percentages of floor area.

Not every trench behaved identically. Heat, geometry, supports, and snow conditions all mattered.

But even the average figures turn a finished city into a continuing construction site.

A corridor must be tall enough for its occupants and equipment. When it loses height, someone has to create that space again.

Maintaining the original openings throughout the camp, the Army estimated, would mean trimming and removing roughly twenty thousand cubic yards of snow per year.

This was a maintenance estimate, not a claim that twenty thousand cubic yards were actually hauled out every year.

Still, picture the implication.

You build the city once.

Then you assign machinery and crews to keep rebuilding its empty spaces.

And the roof is not the only problem.

Blowing snow buried access ramps. Warm air affected parts of the surrounding structure. Some forms had been removed too early. Systems inside the camp produced heat, moisture, and maintenance demands of their own.

Figure thirty-three of the report shows a soldier using an electric chain saw to cut snow away from the eave of a building inside a trench. It looks like an ordinary maintenance task, until you remember what is above the workers.

More snow, under pressure, settling every season.

It is one thing to accept that burden for a scientific test camp.

It is quite another to accept it for a dispersed strategic missile force that must remain ready when needed.

Camp Century demonstrated a way to build in the snow.

Its measurements showed why making that way permanent would be so hard.

And now we reach the part of the story where the Army had to decide what the experiment was still worth.

## CH07 — HOW A CITY DISAPPEARS WITHOUT A DISASTER

<!-- evidence ARMY65_PM2A_P51;AGU16;GAO24;SCI24 -->

There was no final cinematic collapse.

The camp did not disappear beneath an avalanche while soldiers ran for the exit.

Instead, its purpose began to fade.

The Iceworm proposal was rejected.

The nuclear power plant was shut down in July nineteen sixty-three, then dismantled and removed the following year.

A twenty twenty-four scientific history dates year-round occupation to the summer of nineteen sixty-three. Other accounts place the transition in nineteen sixty-four. Seasonal research continued until nineteen sixty-seven.

Then Camp Century was abandoned.

The Army had proved that an underground Arctic settlement could be constructed. But the larger weapons system had failed to justify a path from experiment to deployment.

And shutting down a place under snow is not the same as making it vanish.

The reactor hardware was taken out.

The entire buried infrastructure was not.

Materials, structures, and wastes remained in the ice sheet.

For a long time, the assumption was that snowfall would continue to seal the abandoned site away.

Decades later, scientists began asking whether that assumption would remain reliable as Greenland's climate changed.

One study in twenty sixteen modeled the possibility that surface loss of ice could eventually expose or remobilize buried material under a high-emissions scenario.

But this was a scenario, not an observation of a radioactive river running into the sea.

The United States Government Accountability Office reported in twenty twenty-four that a twenty twenty-one study expected the buried contamination to remain largely immobile through the year twenty-one hundred.

So the scientific question is not resolved by a single dramatic headline.

Denmark established permanent ice-sheet monitoring. Different projections depend on their assumptions about Greenland's future climate.

And there is one last twist.

The Army went to Greenland to learn how to build inside ice that was moving.

When the soldiers left, some of the most valuable discoveries came not from the buildings they abandoned, but from what researchers had drilled out of the ground beneath them.

While Camp Century disappeared from view, something much older was waiting to be found.

## CH08 — THE THING THE ARMY DIDN'T MEAN TO PRESERVE

<!-- evidence SCI24;NASA24;GAO24 -->

In nineteen sixty-six, scientific drilling at Camp Century reached the bottom of the Greenland ice sheet.

Below more than a thousand meters of ice, researchers recovered frozen material from the ancient ground.

At the time, Camp Century was already losing its military future.

But the core samples had a future no one could have fully predicted.

The samples were stored and moved between institutions. Some of the deepest material was long believed lost.

During a twenty nineteen inventory at the University of Copenhagen, a curator located the long-missing subglacial samples and their original records.

It was evidence from below the ice sheet itself.

Later studies found traces of ancient plants and other material associated with environments very different from modern Greenland.

The camp that had been built for a Cold War engineering experiment had preserved a scientific record of a far older landscape.

Then came a new way to see the same abandoned structures — this time from the air.

In April twenty twenty-four, a NASA aircraft was using radar to study Greenland's ice.

The instrument detected the buried structures of Camp Century in a new level of detail. Earlier radar surveys had also detected the base, but this instrument revealed more of its layout.

NASA published the images in November that year.

Not an accessible city filled with working machinery. Not a secret missile complex still operating beneath the glacier.

NASA reported that the structural remains lay at least one hundred feet beneath the surface when described in twenty twenty-four.

That is what makes this ending different from the one in the Army's original film.

The movie shows the day people made a city appear beneath the snow.

The radar shows what remains after the snow has continued moving for sixty years.

And the scientific core reveals that the ice also holds records older than any nation that ever claimed a strategic interest in Greenland.

The engineers built an extraordinary city.

They could not make the material around it stay still.

And the huge missile network for which the experiment might have served as a model never existed.

The lasting evidence is not a hidden arsenal.

It is the film of the men who built the place, the measurements showing how the tunnels changed, and the structures and samples that the ice still holds.

The Army tried to turn Greenland's ice sheet into an enduring military advantage.

What it left behind was an engineering warning, buried in the very material it had hoped to master.
