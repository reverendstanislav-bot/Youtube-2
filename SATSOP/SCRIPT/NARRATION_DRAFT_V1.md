# AMERICA BUILT 74% OF A NUCLEAR PLANT. THEN WALKED AWAY.

Format: composed-narrator documentary
Target runtime: approximately 15–17 minutes
Status: **sourced English V1 — factual pass, editorial review required**

## Hook options

### Hook A — Misconception flip — recommended

These towers look like the remains of one abandoned nuclear plant. They are not. They belong to two reactor projects that stood side by side, used different financial systems, and died twelve years apart. One was stopped at roughly fourteen percent complete. The other reached seventy-four percent. Neither produced a single commercial watt. So why was the more complete reactor still not worth finishing?

### Hook B — Superlative object

This cooling tower rises almost five hundred feet above Washington State. It was designed to serve a 1,240-megawatt nuclear reactor. The reactor was never finished, never fueled, and never operated. Yet the hardest question is not why construction stopped. It is why officials waited until the plant was seventy-four percent complete before deciding that walking away was the cheaper option.

### Hook C — Contradiction autopsy

The usual explanation for Satsop is simple: a giant bond default killed an unfinished nuclear plant. That explanation is also incomplete. The default belonged to one Satsop project. The most complete reactor had a different financial structure and survived for another decade. To understand these towers, we have to separate two projects that history often compresses into one.

## Script

### 【00:00】 Two towers, two endings

[PRESENT-DAY: Static wide shot. Hold the architecture steady. No artificial camera shake.]

Two cooling towers rise from the wooded hills of western Washington.

Each is almost five hundred feet tall. Each was built for a nuclear reactor designed to send roughly 1,240 megawatts into the regional grid.

Neither reactor ever did.

No commercial electricity. No operating plant. The site was never fueled.

[GFX: Split the site into WNP-3 and WNP-5.]

But this is not one abandoned nuclear plant. It is the remains of Washington Nuclear Projects Three and Five — two projects on the same site, built by the same organization, but financed differently and stopped at different moments.

Project Five was about fourteen percent complete when it was terminated in 1982.

Project Three reached seventy-four percent.

And it survived until 1994.

That creates the real mystery of Satsop.

How can a nuclear plant be nearly three-quarters built and still be cheaper to abandon?

### 【01:05】 The future the Northwest expected

[MAP: Pacific Northwest, federal hydro system, Hanford and Satsop sites.]

In the 1970s, the Pacific Northwest was planning for a future of rapidly rising electricity demand.

The Washington Public Power Supply System — WPPSS — was a consortium of publicly owned utilities. It had once been a relatively small organization. Before 1968, according to the U.S. General Accounting Office, it had fewer than one hundred employees.

Then the scale changed.

WPPSS undertook five nuclear projects. Three were at Hanford in eastern Washington. Two were at Satsop, near the Pacific coast.

[ON-SCREEN COUNTER: 5 nuclear projects]

By March 1979, WPPSS employment had reached 1,471. Contemporary plans expected the workforce to grow further as construction advanced.

That expansion reflected the size of the bet. WPPSS was not building one reactor and waiting to see how it performed. At the peak, it was trying to move five large nuclear projects toward completion.

The portfolio itself mattered. A single plant can be delayed. Five projects create overlapping demands for engineers, contractors, regulators, equipment, and debt financing. The same pressure across several projects can change the economics of the entire program.

Only one of the five would eventually operate: WNP-2 at Hanford, which entered commercial service in December 1984. The other four would become different versions of unfinished infrastructure.

Construction at Satsop began in 1977.

The state certification described two pressurized-water reactors. Each unit would have its own turbine generator, reactor buildings, supporting systems, and natural-draft cooling tower.

[GFX: Simplified heat-flow diagram — reactor → steam → turbine → condenser → cooling tower.]

The towers were not reactors. They were the heat-rejection end of a much larger system. In an operating plant, reactor heat would ultimately produce steam, spin a turbine generator, and then be removed from the cycle so water could be used again. The enormous natural-draft towers were designed to move that waste heat into the atmosphere.

That is why they are so visible, even though the most important nuclear machinery would have been enclosed elsewhere.

On paper, they looked like twins.

Financially, they were not.

### 【03:05】 One site, two financial machines

[GFX: Physical map dissolves into two simplified financing diagrams.]

WNP-3 belonged to the group of projects supported through a mechanism called net billing.

Public utilities took shares in the project's generating capability and assigned those shares to the Bonneville Power Administration, the federal agency that marketed power from the Northwest's federal hydroelectric system. Bonneville then credited participating utilities for project costs through their wholesale power bills.

Seventy percent of WNP-3 used this structure. Four investor-owned utilities held the remaining thirty percent. Later agreements changed Bonneville's exposure, but the essential point is simple: WNP-3 had federal power-marketing support behind most of its cost.

WNP-5 followed a different path.

It was financially paired with Project Four at Hanford. Dozens of participating utilities backed municipal bonds through contracts containing a “take or pay” obligation.

The phrase sounds technical. Its effect was not.

Participants promised to pay their share whether the plants were completed, operable, or operating.

[ON SCREEN: PAY — even if no power arrives]

So before either Satsop reactor stopped, the site already contained two different financial experiments.

The structures on the hill concealed that difference. A concrete wall does not show whether its bonds are supported through federal net billing or dozens of local utility contracts. A cooling tower does not reveal who carries the risk if the next round of financing never arrives.

But when costs rose and schedules moved, those invisible terms became more important than the visible similarity of the plants.

That distinction explains why the two units would not die together.

### 【05:30】 The first collapse

[DOCUMENT: GAO Project 4/5 termination chronology.]

By the start of the 1980s, the five-project program was colliding with the limits of the financial market.

On January 22, 1982, WPPSS terminated Projects Four and Five.

The reason documented by GAO was stark: financial advisers concluded that markets could not absorb the bond financing required to carry all five projects.

At that moment, WNP-5 at Satsop was only about fourteen percent complete.

Two years earlier, GAO had reported WNP-5 at roughly eight percent. The physical project was advancing, but the expected price of reaching the finish line was moving much faster.

Projects Four and Five together had already sold 2.25 billion dollars in bonds. WPPSS estimated that completing both would cost roughly twelve billion dollars — about 8.5 billion above the original combined estimate.

Those numbers belong to Projects Four and Five together. They are often pulled out of context and attached to the entire Satsop site. That erases the distinction that matters most.

The take-or-pay structure was challenged in court. On July 22, 1983, the Project Four and Five bonds went into default.

The obligation had been designed to make the bonds dependable: participants would pay even if the projects did not deliver electricity. Once the projects were terminated, that same feature became the center of the dispute. Utilities and ratepayers were being asked to support debt attached to power plants that would never produce the power described in the original plan.

This was more than a construction cancellation. It was a conflict over who should absorb the cost of a future that no longer existed.

[PRESENT-DAY: Unit 5 side, restrained movement.]

That is the famous WPPSS financial disaster.

But it does not finish the story at Satsop.

Because next door, WNP-3 was still alive.

### 【07:55】 Suspended animation

[ARCHIVE/DOCUMENT: WNP-3 construction, authenticated source required.]

Construction on WNP-3 was suspended in July 1983.

Suspended did not mean terminated.

The project was preserved so that work could resume if future demand and economics justified completion. Buildings had to be protected. Equipment and materials retained value. Studies continued.

Preservation was not free, and it was not the same as progress. It bought time.

That time had value because the region did not yet know whether future electricity needs might make completion attractive again. But every year also changed the comparison. Alternative technologies evolved. Financing conditions moved. Rules and projected operating costs changed. Equipment sitting on a dormant site did not become newer.

WNP-3 became infrastructure in suspended animation.

By 1994, Bonneville described the reactor as seventy-four percent physically complete. Approximately 2.6 billion dollars had been invested, measured in 1993 dollars and including the investor-owned utilities' share.

[GFX: Construction meter stops at 74%.]

Seventy-four percent feels close.

The reactor building existed. The turbine building existed. The cooling tower dominated the skyline. From a distance, the remaining question appeared almost mechanical: how much more work would it take to finish?

But that is the wrong question.

Money already spent cannot be recovered by spending more. The decision in 1994 was not whether the first 2.6 billion dollars had been useful.

It was whether the next dollar should go into WNP-3.

That principle is easy to state and difficult to follow. The more concrete is standing, the more completion can feel like recovery. Abandonment makes the loss visible. Continuing construction can postpone the moment when the loss has to be acknowledged.

But past spending cannot lower tomorrow's risk. It can only change what already exists at the starting line of the next decision.

### 【10:25】 The last comparison

[DOCUMENT/GFX: BPA 1994 alternatives. Do not animate unsourced values.]

Bonneville's 1994 Record of Decision compared completing WNP-3 with building combined-cycle generation — power plants that use gas turbines and steam turbines together.

An earlier study estimated another 1.55 billion dollars would be needed to complete WNP-3. But the comparison went beyond a single construction number.

The figure was an estimate, not a guaranteed price. And a power plant does not become useful when the last concrete pour ends. It must pass testing, enter service, operate reliably, manage fuel and waste, and continue earning value long enough to recover the capital committed to it.

Bonneville tested different scenarios.

In the base and high cases, combined-cycle power was cheaper. In the low-cost WNP-3 case, the nuclear project became comparable over its operating life.

Comparable was not enough.

Finishing WNP-3 required committing a much larger block of capital before the plant could earn anything. If the project failed later, far more money would be exposed than with a combined-cycle alternative that could be built in smaller, more flexible increments.

That flexibility changed the meaning of risk. A smaller project could respond to demand as it appeared. WNP-3 required a large decision up front, based on forecasts extending far into the future — the same kind of forecasts that had helped launch the five-project program in the first place.

There were other uncertainties: nuclear decommissioning, waste disposal, regulation, construction, and eventual operation.

WNP-3 also had advantages. It was substantially built. Major site approvals existed. A nuclear plant would not face fuel-combustion limits on carbon dioxide or sulfur dioxide.

Bonneville acknowledged those advantages.

Then it rejected the risk.

In May 1994, the agency decided that the possible benefit of completing WNP-3 did not justify the additional capital and business exposure. Project Three was terminated in June.

[RETURN TO OPENING SHOT: 74% graphic beside the tower.]

This is why seventy-four percent could still be too early to finish.

The number measured concrete and equipment. It did not measure the economic value of the remaining decision.

### 【13:35】 Concrete and contracts keep different time

[TIMELINE: 1982 → 1983 → 1994 → 1999 → FY2000.]

A megaproject does not end on one date.

WNP-5 construction ended with the Project Four and Five termination in 1982. Their bonds defaulted in 1983.

WNP-3 construction had stopped in 1983, but the project decision came in 1994. Its Nuclear Regulatory Commission construction permit was revoked in January 1999.

Then came property, equipment, remediation, and debt.

These followed separate clocks.

The permit clock asked whether construction authority still existed. The property clock asked who would own and maintain the site. The debt clock followed repayment schedules and financing agreements. The redevelopment clock asked what the land and buildings could become next.

Energy Northwest — the later name of WPPSS — was still budgeting for Project Three debt-related costs decades after termination. Its fiscal-year 2021 budget recorded no new construction or operating work on the project, only financing obligations and administration tied to the debt.

That does not mean an operating reactor was being paid for.

It means financial infrastructure can survive long after physical construction stops.

Concrete is visible. Contracts are not.

At Satsop, both remained.

### 【15:15】 A second use for a first failure

[PRESENT-DAY: Active roads, businesses, turbine building, training areas.]

Local officials did not wait for the entire site to disappear.

In 1995, Grays Harbor County, the Port of Grays Harbor, and the local public utility district formed the Satsop Redevelopment Project. The transfer of Project Three property and facilities was completed during fiscal year 2000.

The purpose changed from power generation to economic development.

That shift required a different way to value the site. An unfinished reactor has almost no value as a reactor if nobody will finish it. But the same property may still contain roads, heavy industrial buildings, water infrastructure, electrical systems, transmission access, warehouses, and large areas of developed land.

The original project concentrated these assets in one place. Redevelopment separated them from the failed purpose that had created them.

Today, Satsop is an active, community-owned business park. The old turbine building offers roughly 300,000 square feet of industrial space. Roads, electrical connections, water systems, wastewater facilities, natural gas, and fiber links make the site useful to businesses whose work has nothing to do with nuclear power.

Other spaces found stranger second lives.

Underground tunnels provide realistic confined-space training. Portions of the reactor building are used for rescue and other specialized exercises. Structures built for one of the most controlled industrial environments in America became tools for preparing people to work in other difficult environments.

The change is not a complete economic reversal. A business park does not retroactively repay every nuclear-project cost, and a reused warehouse does not turn an unfinished reactor into a successful power investment.

Reuse answers a narrower question: once the original plan is gone, can any physical value be prevented from disappearing with it?

At Satsop, the answer was yes.

[GFX: ORIGINAL PURPOSE → RETAINED ASSET → CURRENT USE]

This does not erase the failed forecasts, the unfinished construction, or the financial obligations.

It changes what “abandoned” means.

The nuclear projects ended. The industrial site did not.

### 【17:20】 What the towers actually record

[PRESENT-DAY: Return to the opening composition at a different time of day.]

From the highway, the two cooling towers still look like a matched pair.

That is the visual trick at the center of Satsop.

They make two histories look like one.

One unit belonged to Projects Four and Five, the take-or-pay contracts, and the 1983 bond default. The other reached seventy-four percent under a different financing system, then lost a later comparison against a more flexible alternative.

The first story is about the point where financing failed.

The second is about the discipline to ignore sunk cost.

And the business park is about what happens when a community inherits the physical remains of both decisions.

Satsop was never an operating nuclear plant. It was a forecast made physical — in steel, utilities, tunnels, and two cooling towers almost five hundred feet high.

The forecast failed.

The concrete remained.

And concrete can outlive the future that paid for it.
