The gas option had risks, including fuel prices and possible carbon costs. Bonneville recorded those too.

This matters because the 1994 decision was not a claim that combined-cycle power was risk-free or that nuclear generation had no advantages. It was a comparison of which risks Bonneville was willing and able to carry.

But it offered something Project Three could not.

Flexibility.

An earlier study put the base estimate to complete Project Three at another one point five five billion dollars, in 1993 dollars. Bonneville also tested lower and higher cases.

Combined cycle was cheaper than the base and high Project Three cases. In the low-cost case, Project Three became comparable over its operating life.

But comparable lifetime cost was not enough.

In Bonneville's base case, completing Project Three exposed the agency to nearly three times as much capital at risk as the combined-cycle alternative. This comparison measured what would be lost if the resources were terminated early.

Even the low Project Three case carried double the capital at risk.

Then came the need question.

Bonneville projected that Project Three's full twelve hundred and forty-megawatt output would create about six hundred average megawatts of surplus power even under its high-load forecast.

If all that surplus could be sold at Bonneville's Priority Firm rate, the agency still estimated a deficit of roughly two hundred million dollars per year.

The project could work technically and still fail economically. It could generate every megawatt promised and create a financial problem because too much capacity arrived at once.

That is what the cooling towers conceal. Megaprojects are usually judged by whether engineers can finish them. Project Three also had to survive a second test: whether the system around it could use and pay for what it produced.

This was the answer hidden behind the seventy-four-percent number.

The project was mostly built.

The region did not need all of its power.

And finishing it would concentrate far more capital in one decision than the alternative.

Project Three still had real advantages. Much of it existed. Major siting permits were in place. Nuclear generation would not face combustion limits on carbon dioxide or sulfur dioxide.

Bonneville acknowledged those points.

It also listed uncertainty in completion cost, upgrades after years of preservation, operating performance, maintenance, waste, decommissioning, regulation, and public opposition.

Bonneville examined other possibilities. It considered repowering Project Three as a gas-fired combined-cycle plant and found no clear economic advantage over building a new one. It considered whether a third party might use the site. None of those possibilities justified Bonneville continuing to finance preservation for nuclear completion under the existing agreements.

In May 1994, the Bonneville administrator made the final determination. Neither continued preservation nor completion under the existing agreements was in the best interest of Bonneville and the region's ratepayers.

Project Three was terminated in June.

Not because seventy-four percent had disappeared.

Because seventy-four percent described the past. The decision had to describe the future.

A megaproject does not end on a single date.

Project Five was terminated in 1982. The Project Four and Five bonds defaulted in 1983.

Project Three construction stopped in 1983. Its completion option ended in 1994. The Nuclear Regulatory Commission revoked its construction permit in January 1999.

Property, remediation, and debt moved on different schedules.

The permit clock asked whether nuclear construction could legally continue. The property clock asked who would own and maintain the buildings. The debt clock followed financing agreements created years earlier. The redevelopment clock asked whether land and infrastructure designed for one purpose could support another.

Energy Northwest — the later name of the Supply System — still carried Project Three debt-related budgeting decades after termination. Its fiscal-year twenty twenty-one budget covered financing obligations and debt administration, not new construction or plant operation.

Concrete is visible.

Contracts are not.

Both can survive the moment work stops.

In 1995, Grays Harbor County, the Port of Grays Harbor, and the local public utility district formed the Satsop Redevelopment Project.

Transfer of the Project Three property and facilities was completed during fiscal year 2000.

The nuclear investment could not be recovered by pretending the reactor still made sense. But parts of the industrial site had value outside the original plan.
