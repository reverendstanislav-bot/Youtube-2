Two cooling towers rise from the wooded hills of western Washington.

They look like a matched pair. Their histories are not.

One belonged to Washington Nuclear Project Five. That project was about fourteen percent complete when it was terminated in 1982.

The other served Washington Nuclear Project Three. That project reached seventy-four percent physical completion and remained preserved for possible completion until 1994.

Neither project produced commercial electricity. The site was never fueled.

So the mystery is not simply why Satsop was unfinished.

It is why two reactor projects, built side by side, died twelve years apart — and why seventy-four percent complete was still too expensive to finish.

On January 22, 1982, the Washington Public Power Supply System terminated Nuclear Projects Four and Five.

Project Four stood at Hanford. Project Five stood here at Satsop.

Financial advisers had reached a blunt conclusion. The bond market could not absorb the financing needed to carry all five nuclear projects then being pursued by the Supply System.

At termination, Projects Four and Five had sold two point two five billion dollars in bonds. Their estimated combined completion cost had climbed to roughly twelve billion dollars — about eight point five billion above the original combined estimate.

Project Five itself was only about fourteen percent complete.

The physical project advanced. The estimate for completing Projects Four and Five had already moved far beyond the original combined plan.

But this was not just a construction problem.

It was a promise to pay.

To see how the region reached that point, rewind sixteen years.

Through the mid-twentieth century, federal hydroelectric projects supplied most of the Pacific Northwest's electricity. By 1966, planners projected demand growing beyond what that system could provide. Utilities and Bonneville organized a regional planning effort. In 1969, that effort adopted a plan calling for several large thermal plants.

the Supply System moved from a small organization into a builder of five nuclear projects. Before 1968 it had fewer than one hundred employees. By March 1979, it had 1,471.

This was not one isolated plant running over budget. It was an entire portfolio competing for engineering, contractors, equipment, regulatory attention, and financing at the same time.

Only one of those five projects would eventually operate: Project Two at Hanford, which entered commercial service in December 1984.

By July 1976, eighty-eight customers of the Bonneville Power Administration and one private utility had become participants in Projects Four and Five.

The bonds were backed by contracts containing a take-or-pay obligation.

The phrase sounds obscure. Its meaning was painfully clear.

Participants were required to pay their share whether the plants were completed, operable, or operating.

When the projects were alive, that promise helped make their bonds marketable. Once construction ended, it meant utilities could still face the cost of plants that would never deliver power.

GAO reported that rate increases would be required to service the debt. Utilities and ratepayers went to court. The question was no longer only whether the projects should continue. It was who would carry the bill after they stopped.

On July 22, 1983, the Project Four and Five bonds went into default.

That is the famous the Supply System financial collapse.

And yet the neighboring project at Satsop did not end with it.

To understand why, the two towers have to be separated financially.

Project Five belonged to the participant-financed Project Four and Five system.

Project Three belonged to an earlier group supported through net billing with Bonneville, the federal agency that marketed power from the Northwest's federal hydroelectric system.

Public utilities held capability shares in Project Three and assigned them to Bonneville. In exchange, Bonneville credited project costs through their wholesale power bills.

Seventy percent of Project Three used that public net-billing structure. Four investor-owned utilities held the remaining thirty percent. A later settlement changed Bonneville's exposure, and Bonneville ultimately paid all Project Three project costs.

The structures looked similar because both projects were planned as large pressurized-water reactors of roughly twelve hundred and forty megawatts.

But a cooling tower cannot show who carries the next billion dollars of risk.
