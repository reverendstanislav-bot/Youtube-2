Roads. Large buildings. Electrical connections. Water and wastewater systems. Transmission access. Fiber. Developed land.

Those assets had been assembled for a nuclear station, but many were not inherently nuclear. A heavy industrial building can house manufacturing. A tunnel can become a controlled training environment. Electrical and water capacity can attract tenants that could never justify building the same infrastructure from scratch.

Today the site operates as a community-owned business park.

The former turbine building offers roughly three hundred thousand square feet of industrial space.

Underground tunnels support confined-space training. Portions of the reactor building are used for rescue and other specialized exercises.

This reuse does not repay every nuclear-project cost. It does not turn Project Three or Project Five into successful power investments.

Nor does it make the restricted nuclear structures public attractions. The Port warns visitors to remain on public roads and outside fenced areas. The business park is a working industrial site, not an abandoned playground.

It answers a narrower question.

After the original future disappears, can the infrastructure do anything useful next?

At Satsop, some of it could.

The towers still look like twins.

Now they record two different failures.

Project Five marks the point where one financing system broke.

Project Three marks the point where sunk cost lost to future risk.

The power never arrived.

The infrastructure found another job.
