## Red findings — required before V2 fact-check

### R1 — “The reactor was 74 percent complete” is imprecise

BPA says **WNP-3 was 74 percent complete** and describes physical construction at the project level. The narration repeatedly turns this into a percentage of “the reactor.” A nuclear project includes far more than the reactor itself.

Required wording:

> The WNP-3 project was seventy-four percent physically complete.

Use this in the hook, body, title explanation, and all graphics.

### R2 — The title implies an abrupt exit that did not happen

`America Built 74% of a Nuclear Plant. Then Walked Away.` promises a sudden abandonment. The actual project was suspended in 1983, preserved for possible completion for roughly eleven years, and terminated in 1994.

Recommended title:

> **Why a 74%-Complete Nuclear Plant Was Still Too Expensive to Finish**

More cinematic alternative:

> **They Built 74% of a Nuclear Plant — Then the Math Collapsed**

The second is acceptable only if the film immediately explains that “the math” means BPA's forward-looking cost and risk comparison.

### R3 — The climax omits BPA's most powerful proof

The 1994 record supplies two decisive comparisons absent from V1:

- WNP-3 exposed Bonneville to nearly **three times** the capital at risk versus equivalent combined-cycle energy in the base case, and double in the low case.
- The full 1,240 MW project was projected to create roughly **600 average megawatts of surplus** even under BPA's high-load forecast, producing an estimated **$200 million annual deficit** if sold at the Priority Firm rate.

These figures answer the title question more concretely than general references to “risk.” They belong at the 70–80 percent convergence point, with precise scope and 1994 attribution.

### R4 — One comparative sentence outruns its evidence

V1 says WNP-5's physical project advanced while “the expected price of reaching the finish line was moving much faster.” The underlying sources show rising costs and progress percentages, but the script does not calculate comparable rates over the same period.

Replace with:

> The project advanced. The estimate for completing Projects Four and Five had already moved far beyond the original combined plan.

### R5 — The actual runtime is not yet the timestamped runtime

The script contains about 2,200–2,350 spoken words depending on extraction rules. At 140–145 words per minute this is roughly 15–17 minutes before measured pauses. The final section is labeled 17:20 without an audio authority.

Rule: treat all V1 timestamps as structural placeholders. TTS becomes the only runtime authority.

## Yellow findings — high-priority directorial revision

### Y1 — No human carries the first ten minutes

The active characters are WPPSS, BPA, GAO, bonds, projects, and markets. Ratepayers and workers appear as categories rather than people. This keeps the film intellectually clear but emotionally remote.

V2 needs at least one authenticated human-scale thread:

- a worker or project engineer confronting preservation;
- a participating utility/ratepayer facing take-or-pay obligations;
- a local redevelopment official explaining what the inherited site could become.

No quote may be invented. If a cleared voice cannot be found, use one documented meeting, decision, invoice, or physical maintenance task as the human-scale proxy.

### Y2 — The first act waits too long for conflict

From approximately 01:05 through 05:30, the film explains regional demand, five projects, reactor systems, net billing, investor-owned shares, and take-or-pay. This is necessary information delivered in a long block.

V2 order:

1. show the 1982 Project 4/5 termination within the first three minutes;
2. ask why the neighboring WNP-3 did not die with it;
3. explain the financing split as the answer to that mystery.

Conflict should force the explanation. Explanation should not delay the conflict.

### Y3 — The language becomes abstract in clusters

Repeated abstract nouns include `project`, `financing`, `construction`, `risk`, `decision`, and `infrastructure`. This is unavoidable in moderation, but several passages stack them without a physical object or action.

For every 30–45 seconds of institutional explanation, V2 needs one of:

- a tower, permit page, bond document, switchyard, empty turbine floor, sealed opening, preserved component, map move, invoice, or calendar jump;
- a specific decision somebody had to make;
- a visual comparison whose meaning changes.

### Y4 — The combined-cycle opponent is under-visualized

Combined cycle currently arrives as a definition and remains an abstraction. The audience needs to see why it changed the decision:

- 240 MW reference unit versus 1,240 MW WNP-3;
- three-year versus five-year assumed lead time in BPA's exhibits;
- smaller increments;
- more variable/avoidable costs;
- lower up-front capital exposure.

This should be a clean animated comparison, not stock footage of gas turbines.

### Y5 — The ending repeats the thesis too many times

The last section restates two histories, financing failure, sunk cost, reuse, failed forecast, remaining concrete, and the final aphorism. The meaning is strong; the landing is over-explained.

Cut 20–30 percent from the ending. Keep the matched-tower reinterpretation and one final line.

## Visual audit

### Current strengths

- steady present-day architecture is explicitly preferred over artificial shake;
- site map, financial diagrams, construction meter, decision document, timeline, and reuse matrix provide distinct visual modes;
- the script avoids treating generated reconstruction as archive.

### Current weaknesses

- only about seventeen broad production cues cover the entire film;
- several cues name a category rather than an executable shot;
- the longest explanatory runs would default to documents plus slow zooms;
- archive rights remain unresolved;
- no authenticated diagram yet separates Unit 3, Unit 5, and shared systems;
- no shot-level reason exists for returning to a given tower beyond atmosphere.

### V2 visual rule

Every visual beat must perform one job:

1. establish place or scale;
2. prove a claim;
3. explain a mechanism;
4. show a consequence;
5. change the meaning of an earlier image.

If a shot performs none of these jobs, it is wallpaper and should be removed.

## Narration audit

### Strong lines

- “On paper, they looked like twins. Financially, they were not.”
- “Preservation was not free, and it was not the same as progress. It bought time.”
- “Concrete is visible. Contracts are not.”
- “The nuclear projects ended. The industrial site did not.”

### Lines to reconsider

- “two different financial experiments” — sounds editorialized; use “two different financing structures.”
- “one of the most controlled industrial environments in America” — unsupported superlative and generic flourish.
- “an unfinished reactor has almost no value as a reactor” — circular and broader than the evidence.
- “the discipline to ignore sunk cost” — correct thesis, but sounds like a management lesson unless grounded in the BPA numbers immediately before it.
- “the future that paid for it” — the future did not pay; utilities, BPA, investors, and ratepayers did.

Recommended final-line territory:

> The forecasts disappeared. Their concrete did not.

or

> The power never arrived. The infrastructure found another job.
