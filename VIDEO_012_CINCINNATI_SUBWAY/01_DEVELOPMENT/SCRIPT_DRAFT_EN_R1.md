## ACT 1 — THE WRONG MACHINE

### A01-P01

In 1957, workers entered a subway tunnel beneath Cincinnati and began installing something almost four feet wide. It was not a rail. It was not a train. It was a reinforced-concrete pipe—part of a water system being threaded through a transit system that had never carried a single passenger.

### A01-P02

Above them, traffic moved along Central Parkway. Below, more than two miles of concrete tunnel and unfinished stations waited in the dark. Cincinnati had done the most expensive part: it had excavated the route, poured the structure and buried it beneath a new boulevard. But it had never installed the machinery that would make the structure a subway.

### A01-P03

That is the paradox beneath this street. The project was not a drawing that failed to leave City Hall. Much of it was physically built. The question is how a city could finish the tunnels, stop before the trains, and spend the next century trying to make the empty structure useful.

Nonspoken evidence: F01, F02, F11. Opening visual priority is the authentic 1957 GCWW film, subject to item-level rights clearance; fallback is a sourced tunnel plan plus present Central Parkway exterior.

## ACT 2 — A RAILWAY IN A CANAL

### A02-P01

The route began as water. The Miami and Erie Canal once entered Cincinnati along the corridor now occupied by Central Parkway. By the late nineteenth century, the canal had lost much of its value. For transit planners, however, its depressed right-of-way offered something rare inside a growing city: a path where a railway could be built without cutting a completely new corridor through blocks of private property.

### A02-P02

Engineers studied several versions of a rapid-transit loop. The ambitious plans were too expensive, so the city reduced them to a six-million-dollar program that mixed tunnels, open cuts, surface track and elevated sections. On April 25, 1916, Cincinnati voters approved the bond issue by almost six to one.

### A02-P03

The vote looked decisive. The timing was not. The United States entered the First World War, capital issues were restricted, and construction could not proceed as planned. When contracts finally moved forward after the war, the same six million dollars had to buy labor and materials in a more expensive economy.

Nonspoken evidence: F03–F06. Do not illustrate the full loop as completed; map must distinguish planned, constructed tunnel, constructed right-of-way and never-built sections.

## ACT 3 — CONCRETE WITHOUT A SYSTEM

### A03-P01

Work began with contracts at the end of 1919 and major construction in 1920. The old canal bed became a construction trench. Crews built twin tubes and stations, then covered sections of the work so a new parkway could run above them. By early 1923, the principal tunnel work was finished. Aboveground portions continued for several more years.

### A03-P02

From the street, this could look like progress toward an inevitable opening. Underground, the difference between a tunnel and an operating railway was becoming impossible to ignore. There were no tracks. There was no electrical or signaling equipment. Critical connections and right-of-way were still missing. Cincinnati had built a shell, not a functioning network.

### A03-P03

That distinction explains why photographs of the tunnel can be misleading. The concrete appears complete because much of the civil structure was complete. But a rapid-transit line works only when every required system connects: route, rails, power, control, stations, vehicles and access. A finished tube with a broken route is not a partial subway. Operationally, it is no subway at all.

### A03-P04

By 1927, the bond money was exhausted. The Rapid Transit Commission estimated that saving the project would require roughly another nine to ten million dollars. A contemporary city directory described about seven million dollars more in construction work, excluding equipment. Different estimates measured different scopes, but they agreed on the essential fact: the original fund could no longer finish the system.

Nonspoken evidence: F07–F10. The differing completion estimates must remain attributed and must not be collapsed into one false exact figure.

## ACT 4 — WHY IT STOPPED

### A04-P01

It is tempting to give an abandoned megaproject one dramatic cause. Cincinnati's subway does not offer that simplicity. Inflation had reduced what the 1916 bond could purchase. The proposed loop still lacked key property and connections. Political reform changed who controlled the project and how it was judged. Meanwhile, the city's growth and transportation market were changing.

### A04-P02

Mayor Murray Seasongood and other reformers challenged the county-controlled rapid-transit board. The board was eventually eliminated. Its final report warned that completing the line would require another huge commitment. Then the Great Depression destroyed the financial setting in which such a commitment might have been made.

### A04-P03

Central Parkway opened in 1928 over part of the abandoned structure. Cars now traveled above the route intended to compete with street congestion below. The tunnel did not vanish. It simply became infrastructure without an operating institution, a budget or a complete network behind it.

### A04-P04

The original six-million-dollar bond remained on the city's books until 1966. According to Cincinnati's own historical account, taxes and interest brought the final cost to exactly $13,019,982.45. For half a century, residents were still paying for a railway that had never earned a fare.

Nonspoken evidence: F12–F15. Avoid implying that automobiles alone killed the project; the evidence supports a multi-cause failure.

## ACT 5 — A TUNNEL LOOKING FOR WORK

### A05-P01

Once the railway plan was dead, the concrete invited new ideas. Engineers proposed linking the tubes to streetcars, but some cars could not negotiate the curves. Another proposal imagined automobiles using an extended underground route to reach riverfront parking. After the Second World War, a freight plan reached the lease stage before its cars were found not to fit.

### A05-P02

These were not merely eccentric failures. They revealed the cost of adapting specialized infrastructure. The tunnel's dimensions, curves, entrances and missing connections had been designed for one system. Reusing the shell for another purpose could require almost as many compromises as completing the original railway.

### A05-P03

During the Cold War, one station received equipment for use by government personnel during a fallout emergency. Later proposals included storage, commercial space and new transit concepts. The abandoned subway became a recurring civic blank screen: every generation could imagine a use, but imagination did not remove the engineering constraints.

### A05-P04

One often-repeated story says Hollywood considered the tunnel for Batman Forever. That may explain part of the site's modern mythology, but it is not the central evidence. The more consequential second life was already inside the west tube—and it was carrying water.

Nonspoken evidence: F16–F18. A05-P04 remains HOLD until primary Batman evidence is found; delete it rather than overstate it if not resolved.

## ACT 6 — THE SUBWAY THAT CARRIED WATER

### A06-P01

In 1957, Cincinnati Water Works installed a 48-inch reinforced-concrete transmission main through about two miles of the rapid-transit tunnel. Historical city footage records the equipment and methods used to move the large pipe sections underground. The empty subway had finally become part of a working citywide system—just not the one voters had authorized in 1916.

### A06-P02

The reuse was practical because the tunnel offered a protected, continuous corridor. But it also made future transit reuse more complicated. A large water main and other utilities occupy space, require access and cannot simply be treated as temporary props in an abandoned room.

### A06-P03

This is where the story turns again. City budget documents say the old subway-tunnel mains now contain sections needing replacement or repair. A new Beekman transmission-main project is intended to let Greater Cincinnati Water Works abandon the aging mains inside the tunnel. The infrastructure that gave the subway its second life is itself approaching the end of that role.

Nonspoken evidence: F19–F21. “Other utilities” must remain generic until the current inventory is verified. Do not identify carriers or routes.

## ACT 7 — THE QUESTION RETURNS

### A07-P01

Removing the water function does not automatically restore a railway. The tunnel still has limited connections, old geometry, structural interfaces with the city above and a century of changed safety standards. A useful future would have to begin with inspection and engineering, not a render of trains inside an empty tube.

### A07-P02

In 2025, Cincinnati listed and awarded a Subway Tunnel Feasibility Study. The wording matters. This is not an approved new subway and not a promised entertainment district. It is a study of what the surviving structure can safely and realistically support.

### A07-P03

At the same time, the tunnel still affects projects on the surface. A replacement for the deteriorated Brighton Approach Bridge was designed in part to eliminate support resting on the abandoned subway. Even unused infrastructure continues to impose loads, clearances and costs on the living city around it.

Nonspoken evidence: F22–F24. Obtain the full feasibility-study scope before narrating specific reuse options.

## ACT 8 — WHAT CINCINNATI ACTUALLY BUILT

### A08-P01

Cincinnati did not build a subway and then forget to open the doors. It built one expensive layer of a much larger system, while the money, route and political continuity needed for the other layers disappeared. The concrete survived because concrete can outlast the plan that produced it.

### A08-P02

That survival created both value and burden. The tubes carried water. Their right-of-way helped shape later roads. Their presence still changes bridge design and utility planning. But every reuse inherits the geometry and unfinished decisions of 1916.

### A08-P03

The real monument beneath Central Parkway is not simply to waste. It is to the difference between building an object and completing a system. A tunnel can be poured, covered and preserved for a century. Until all the connected parts exist, it still goes nowhere.

### A08-P04

Now that Cincinnati is studying the tunnel again, what should decide its future: the original promise of transit, the practical value of another use, or the cost of leaving it alone?
