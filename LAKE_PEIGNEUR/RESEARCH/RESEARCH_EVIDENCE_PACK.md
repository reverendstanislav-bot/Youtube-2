# Lake Peigneur — Research Evidence Pack V1

Updated: 2026-09-24

## 1. The event

On **November 20, 1980**, Lake Peigneur at Jefferson Island, Louisiana, rapidly drained into underground salt-mine workings. A Texaco oil exploration well was being drilled from a platform on the lake by Wilson Brothers while Diamond Crystal Salt Company operated the mine below.

The drilling assembly reportedly became stuck at approximately **1,228 ft**. The drilling crew abandoned the platform before it disappeared as the lake began draining.

## 2. Why the failure could accelerate

The central physical mechanism is unusually cinematic and should be explained clearly:

1. a pathway opened between surface water and salt-mine workings;
2. freshwater entered the mine;
3. water dissolves rock salt;
4. widening flow paths and loss/collapse of salt structure could admit still more water;
5. the lake therefore did not behave like water entering a fixed-diameter pipe — the failure could enlarge itself.

Do not over-animate impossible underground geometry. The precise flooded pathway was not directly inspectable afterward.

## 3. The mine evacuation

The strongest human chapter is not a casualty story; it is a successful emergency evacuation.

MSHA's FY1981 reporting states:
- **52 people were underground**;
- the inflow was discovered by a mine electrician;
- all were evacuated safely;
- evacuation was completed in **less than one hour**;
- training and emergency procedures were credited as crucial.

This should be staged as action, not summarized in one sentence.

## 4. Surface collapse

Contemporaneous reporting documented:
- a large whirlpool/crater;
- the drilling platform disappearing;
- barges, boats, trees and shoreline being pulled toward the failure;
- evacuations around Jefferson Island;
- no human injuries reported.

Exact counts of barges/land acreage should be sourced in the final claim map rather than casually repeated.

## 5. The canal reversal

As the lake level collapsed, the normal flow of the **Delcambre Canal** reversed.

Water from the canal / Vermilion Bay moved toward Lake Peigneur. Louisiana historical sources describe a temporary waterfall roughly **150 ft** high as water poured toward the drained basin.

This is the visual midpoint/climax:
**the lake falls underground, then the Gulf begins running backward to refill it.**

## 6. Why the popular cause story is too neat

Many retellings say the disaster was simply caused by a coordinate or surveying error.

That may be a plausible reconstruction, but the federal MSHA investigation did not close the case that neatly. It said the mine could not be inspected and the available evidence was circumstantial; the precise cause was therefore extremely difficult to determine.

The film should distinguish:
- **documented mechanism:** drilling operation + pathway + mine inundation;
- **contested allocation of fault:** exact drill location, mine mapping, communication and responsibility.

This distinction makes the film stronger, not weaker.

## 7. Litigation and economic consequences

The accident destroyed the mine as an operating industrial system and triggered extensive litigation.

Documented:
- Diamond Crystal originally sought $260 million.
- In 1983 it accepted a **$32 million** settlement from Texaco and Wilson Brothers.
- Live Oak Gardens interests received **$12.8 million** in another settlement involving Texaco, Wilson Brothers and Diamond Crystal.
- Louisiana appellate litigation records approximately **250 mine employees** being laid off.
- Employee claims proceeded separately.

## 8. Physical legacy

The event permanently changed Lake Peigneur and the Jefferson Island shoreline.

Exact post-event depth and exact land-loss acreage remain **numbers-to-close** before narration.

## 9. Present-day industrial epilogue

The salt dome did not leave industrial history after 1980.

Federal and pipeline records show **Jefferson Island Storage & Hub** remains part of the natural-gas storage / pipeline system, and a 2025 USACE public notice concerned proposed lake work tied to storage-cavern infrastructure.

Possible ending:
The same geology that made salt mining possible — and made the 1980 failure catastrophic — remains industrially valuable today.
